The Big Picture: Why We Factor

In Unit 1, we learned how to analyze the outer edges of a polynomial graph using degree and leading coefficients (End Behaviour).

However, to understand what a graph does in the middle, we need to know exactly where it crosses the x-axis.

The standard form P(x) = ax^n + bx^{n-1} + ... + c does not reveal the x-intercepts.

We must convert it to factored form: P(x) = a(x - r_1)(x - r_2)... where r_1, r_2 are the roots.

The theorems in this section are the tools we use to break down the standard form.

Concept 1: The Remainder Theorem

Polynomial division (long division or synthetic division) allows us to break down an expression, but it can be time-consuming.

If a problem only requires the remainder of a division, we can bypass the division process entirely.

The Remainder Theorem:

When a polynomial P(x) is divided by (x - b), the remainder is exactly equal to P(b).

To find the remainder, simply find the value that makes the divisor equal to zero, and substitute that value into the polynomial.

Worked Example: Remainder Theorem

Problem: What is the remainder when x^4 - 4x^2 - 2x + 3 is divided by x + 1?

Step 1: Identify the Divisor Root

The divisor is x + 1.

Set the divisor to zero: x + 1 = 0 \rightarrow x = -1.

According to the theorem, the remainder will be P(-1).

Worked Example: Remainder Theorem

Step 2: Evaluate the Polynomial

Substitute -1 into the polynomial.

P(-1) = (-1)4 - 4(-1)2 - 2(-1) + 3

P(-1) = 1 - 4(1) + 2 + 3

P(-1) = 1 - 4 + 2 + 3

P(-1) = 2

The remainder is exactly 2.

Checkpoint: Remainder Theorem

Let's verify your understanding of substitution and signs.

Concept 2: The Factor Theorem

In arithmetic, if you divide 12 by 4, the remainder is 0. This tells us that 4 is a perfect factor of 12.

The exact same logic applies to polynomials.

The Factor Theorem:

A binomial x - b is a factor of a polynomial P(x) if and only if P(b) = 0.

This is a specific, highly useful case of the Remainder Theorem. A remainder of zero confirms a factor.

Worked Example: The Factor Theorem

Problem: Is x - 3 a factor of the polynomial 3x^2 - 8x - 3?

To determine this without dividing, we evaluate the polynomial at x = 3.

P(3) = 3(3)2 - 8(3) - 3

P(3) = 3(9) - 24 - 3

P(3) = 27 - 24 - 3

P(3) = 0

Because the remainder is 0, we conclude that yes, x - 3 is a factor.

Concept 3: Finding Unknown Coefficients

A common application of these theorems is working backwards.

You may be given a polynomial with an unknown coefficient (like k) and told what the remainder is.

By applying the Remainder Theorem, you can construct an algebraic equation and isolate the unknown variable.

Worked Example: Finding 'k'

Problem: Determine the value of k such that when f(x) = x^4 + kx^3 - 3x - 5 is divided by x - 3, the remainder is -10.

Step 1: Apply the Remainder Theorem

The divisor is x - 3, so we evaluate at x = 3.

We are told the remainder is -10. Therefore, we establish the equation:

f(3) = -10

Worked Example: Finding 'k'

Step 2: Substitute and Solve

(3)4 + k(3)3 - 3(3) - 5 = -10

81 + 27k - 9 - 5 = -10

27k + 67 = -10

27k = -10 - 67

27k = -77

k = -77 / 27

Fractions are valid answers! The value of k is -77/27.

Concept 4: Systems of Equations

What happens if a polynomial is missing two coefficients?

If you have two unknown variables (e.g., m and n), you need two distinct pieces of information to solve the problem.

This will allow you to generate two linear equations and solve them using substitution or elimination, integrating Grade 10 concepts into Grade 12 polynomials.

Worked Example: Systems of Equations

Problem: Suppose the cubic polynomial 8x^3 + mx^2 + nx - 6 has both 2x + 3 and x - 1 as factors. Find m and n.

We are given two factors, which means we have two roots that will yield a remainder of zero.

  • Factor 1: x - 1 \rightarrow Root is 1
  • Factor 2: 2x + 3 \rightarrow Root is -3/2

Worked Example: System Setup

Equation 1 (using root 1):

P(1) = 0
8(1)3 + m(1)2 + n(1) - 6 = 0
8 + m + n - 6 = 0
m + n + 2 = 0
m + n = -2      (Eq. 1)

Equation 2 (using root -3/2):

P(-3/2) = 0
8(-3/2)3 + m(-3/2)2 + n(-3/2) - 6 = 0
8(-27/8) + m(9/4) - 1.5n - 6 = 0
-27 + 2.25m - 1.5n - 6 = 0
2.25m - 1.5n = 33      (Eq. 2)

Worked Example: System Solution

We solve the system using substitution or elimination.

From Eq 1: n = -2 - m

Substitute into Eq 2:

2.25m - 1.5(-2 - m) = 33
2.25m + 3 + 1.5m = 33
3.75m = 30
m = 8

Substitute m = 8 back into Eq 1:

8 + n = -2
n = -10

Concept 5: Factoring from Scratch

If you are asked to factor a polynomial completely but are given no factors to start with, you must find the first factor yourself.

The Integral Zero Theorem:

If a polynomial with integer coefficients has a rational root, that root must be a factor of the constant term.

You list the factors of the constant term and use the Factor Theorem (guess and check) until you find one that yields a remainder of 0.

Worked Example: Factoring a Cubic

Problem: Factor x^3 - 4x^2 + x + 6 completely.

The constant term is 6. Possible factors: ±1, ±2, ±3, ±6.

Test values until P(x) = 0:

  • P(1) = 1 - 4 + 1 + 6 = 4 ≠ 0
  • P(-1) = -1 - 4 - 1 + 6 = 0. Bingo!

Since P(-1) = 0, we know that (x + 1) is a factor.

Worked Example: Factoring a Cubic

Now we use synthetic division to divide the original cubic by the known factor (x + 1).

The root outside is -1. The coefficients are 1, -4, 1, 6.

-1 |   1    -4     1     6
   |      -1     5    -6
-------------------------
     1    -5     6  |  0

The quotient is the remaining quadratic: x^2 - 5x + 6.

Worked Example: Factoring a Cubic

We have reduced the cubic to:

P(x) = (x + 1)(x2 - 5x + 6)

Factor the resulting quadratic using standard trinomial factoring (find two numbers that multiply to 6 and add to -5). Those numbers are -2 and -3.

P(x) = (x + 1)(x - 2)(x - 3)

The polynomial is now completely factored.

Concept 6: Factoring by Grouping

Occasionally, you can bypass the Integral Zero Theorem and Synthetic Division entirely if the polynomial follows a specific structural pattern.

Factoring by Grouping involves pairing terms together and extracting a common factor from each pair.

If the remaining binomial in both pairs matches exactly, you can factor that binomial out of the entire expression.

Worked Example: Factoring by Grouping

Problem: Factor -4x^3 - 4x^2 + 16x + 16.

First, always look for a greatest common factor (GCF) across the entire expression. We can extract a -4.

-4(x3 + x2 - 4x - 4)

Now, look at the four terms inside the bracket. Group the first two together, and the last two together.

x3 + x2    and    -4x - 4

Worked Example: Factoring by Grouping

Extract the GCF from each pair independently:

  • From x^3 + x^2, extract x^2 → x^2(x + 1)
  • From -4x - 4, extract -4 → -4(x + 1)

Reassemble the expression:

-4[ x2(x + 1) - 4(x + 1) ]

Since (x + 1) is common to both terms, factor it out:

-4(x + 1)(x2 - 4)

Recognize x^2 - 4 as a difference of squares to finish:

-4(x + 1)(x - 2)(x + 2)

Concept 7: Sum and Difference of Cubes

Just as a "difference of squares" (a^2 - b^2) has a set factoring pattern, the sum and difference of cubes have specific, memorizable formulas.

Sum of Cubes:
a^3 + b^3 = (a + b)(a^2 - ab + b^2)


Difference of Cubes:
a^3 - b^3 = (a - b)(a^2 + ab + b^2)

Remember the signs using SOAP for the three operators: Same, Opposite, Always Positive.

Worked Example: Difference of Cubes

Problem: Factor x^3 - 64.

Identify the cubic components. x is being cubed, and 4 is being cubed (4^3 = 64).

So, a = x and b = 4.

Apply the Difference of Cubes formula: (a - b)(a^2 + ab + b^2)

(x - 4)(x2 + 4x + 16)

Note: The quadratic portion of a sum/difference of cubes cannot be factored further using real numbers.

Concept 8: Solving Polynomial Equations

Solving a polynomial equation means finding the values of x that make the equation true. On a graph, these correspond to the x-intercepts.

The Golden Rule for Solving:

  1. Move all terms to one side so the equation is equal to 0.
  2. Factor the polynomial completely.
  3. Set each individual factor equal to 0 and solve for x.

Worked Example: Solving Equations

Problem: Solve 2x^3 + 1 = x^2 + 2x.

Step 1: Move all terms to the left side.

2x3 - x2 - 2x + 1 = 0

Step 2: This polynomial has four terms and looks like a perfect candidate for factoring by grouping.

Worked Example: Solving Equations

Group the terms: (2x^3 - x^2) + (-2x + 1) = 0

x2(2x - 1) - 1(2x - 1) = 0

(2x - 1)(x2 - 1) = 0

(2x - 1)(x - 1)(x + 1) = 0

Step 3: Set each factor to zero to find the roots.

  • 2x - 1 = 0 \rightarrow x = 1/2
  • x - 1 = 0 \rightarrow x = 1
  • x + 1 = 0 \rightarrow x = -1

Concept 9: Identifying Non-Real Roots

Not every factor of a polynomial will yield a "real" root that appears on a Cartesian graph.

If a factor contains a quadratic expression that cannot be factored, you must use the quadratic formula.

If the value under the square root (the discriminant, b^2 - 4ac) is negative, that portion of the equation produces no real solutions.

Worked Example: Non-Real Roots

Problem: Determine the real roots of (5x^2 + 20)(3x^2 - 48) = 0.

The equation is already partially factored. Set each bracket to zero.

Bracket 1:

5x^2 + 20 = 0
5x^2 = -20
x^2 = -4
Bracket 2:

3x^2 - 48 = 0
3x^2 = 48
x^2 = 16

Worked Example: Non-Real Roots

Evaluate the results from the previous step:

  • Bracket 1 (x^2 = -4): You cannot take the square root of a negative number in the real number system. This produces No Real Solutions.
  • Bracket 2 (x^2 = 16): Taking the square root yields two real solutions. x = ±4.

The only real roots for the entire equation are x = 4 and x = -4.

You have factored a polynomial down to P(x) = (x - 2)(x^2 + 3x + 5).

Concept 10: Families of Polynomials

A "Family" of polynomial functions is a set of equations that share the exact same roots (x-intercepts).

While they pass through the same points on the x-axis, they look different because they have different stretch or compression factors.

y = k(x - r1)(x - r2)...(x - rn)

The variable k represents any real number (except 0). Changing k generates different members of the family.

Concept 11: Irrational Roots

If a polynomial has real coefficients, any irrational roots (roots with square roots in them) must appear in conjugate pairs.

If a + \sqrt{b} is a root, then a - \sqrt{b} must also be a root.

To build a family equation using these roots, we work backwards from the quadratic formula to reconstruct the quadratic factor.

Worked Example: Irrational Roots

Problem: Find the equation for the family of quartic polynomials that have roots of 3 (order 2) and 2 ± \sqrt{2}.

The root at 3 with order 2 is simple: (x - 3)^2.

For the irrational roots, we set x equal to the roots and manipulate the equation to remove the square root.

x = 2 ± \sqrt{2}

Worked Example: Reconstructing the Quadratic

Start with: x = 2 ± \sqrt{2}

Move the constant to the left side:

x - 2 = ± \sqrt{2}

Square both sides to eliminate the radical:

(x - 2)2 = 2

Expand and simplify:

x2 - 4x + 4 = 2
x2 - 4x + 2 = 0

We have found our quadratic factor!

Worked Example: The Family Equation

We combine the factors to write the general equation for the entire family of polynomials.

  • Factor 1: (x - 3)^2
  • Factor 2: (x^2 - 4x + 2)
y = k(x - 3)2(x2 - 4x + 2)

This equation represents an infinite number of quartic curves that all share these specific roots.

Worked Example: Finding a Specific Member

Problem: A family of cubic polynomials has roots of -2, -3, and -5. Find the specific member of this family that passes through the point (2, -35).

First, establish the family skeleton:

y = k(x + 2)(x + 3)(x + 5)

To find the specific member, we must solve for k by substituting the known point x = 2 and y = -35.

Worked Example: Solving for k

Substitute the point (2, -35) into the skeleton:

-35 = k(2 + 2)(2 + 3)(2 + 5)

-35 = k(4)(5)(7)

-35 = k(140)

k = -35 / 140

k = -1/4

The specific equation is: y = -1/4(x + 2)(x + 3)(x + 5)

Concept 12: Polynomial Inequalities

An equation asks: Where does the graph cross the x-axis? (y = 0)

An inequality asks: Where is the graph above the x-axis? (> 0) Or where is it below? (< 0)

There are two main methods to solve an inequality:

  1. Algebraically: Using a factor table (Sign Chart).
  2. Graphically: Sketching the curve based on roots and end behavior.

Never divide an inequality by a variable expression, as you do not know if it is positive or negative (which would require flipping the inequality sign).

Worked Example: The Sign Chart

Problem: Solve 2x^3 - x^2 - 2x + 1 < 0.

We factored this exact expression earlier using grouping:

(2x - 1)(x - 1)(x + 1) < 0

The roots are -1, 1/2, and 1.

These roots divide the x-axis into four test intervals. We pick a random number inside each interval to test the signs.

Worked Example: Sign Chart Intervals

Interval Test Point Sign of (2x-1)(x-1)(x+1)
x < -1 -2 (-) × (-) × (-) = Negative
-1 < x < 1/2 0 (-) × (-) × (+) = Positive
1/2 < x < 1 0.75 (+) × (-) × (+) = Negative
x > 1 2 (+) × (+) × (+) = Positive

Worked Example: Sign Chart Evaluation

Our original inequality was strictly less than zero: < 0.

Therefore, we are looking for the intervals that resulted in a Negative sign.

Looking at our chart, those intervals are:

  • x < -1
  • 1/2 < x < 1

In proper interval notation, the solution is:

x ∈ (-∞, -1) ∪ (0.5, 1)

Concept 13: Graphing Inequalities

If you prefer a visual approach, you can sketch the graph instead of building a sign chart.

To do this, you need:

  • The factored form (to plot the x-intercepts).
  • The multiplicity of each root (to know if it crosses or bounces).
  • The degree and leading coefficient (to determine end behaviour).

Once sketched, simply highlight the sections of the curve that satisfy the inequality (above or below the axis).

Worked Example: Graphing Inequalities

Problem: Solve 6x^3 + 13x^2 - 41x + 12 ≤ 0.

Assume we used the Factor Theorem and Synthetic Division to factor this completely into:

(x + 4)(2x - 3)(3x - 1) ≤ 0

The roots are -4, 3/2, and 1/3. All have a multiplicity of 1, so the graph will cross straight through the axis at all three points.

Worked Example: Graphing Evaluation

The degree is 3 (Odd) and the leading coefficient is 6 (Positive). Therefore, the graph starts in Quadrant 3 (bottom left) and ends in Quadrant 1 (top right).

Visualizing the curve:

  • It comes up from the bottom and crosses at -4 (curve is below axis).
  • It loops over and crosses down at 1/3 (curve is above axis).
  • It loops under and crosses up at 3/2 (curve is below axis).
  • It shoots off to infinity (curve is above axis).

We want where it is ≤ 0 (below the axis). That is before -4, and between 1/3 and 3/2.

x ∈ (-∞, -4] ∪ [1/3, 3/2]
