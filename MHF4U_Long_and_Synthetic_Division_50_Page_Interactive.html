Long Division & Synthetic Division

Today we are focusing on two ways to divide polynomials, plus a shortcut for finding remainders.

By the end

You should be able to divide a polynomial, write the quotient and remainder correctly, check the result, find a remainder without dividing, and use synthetic division when it fits.

Watch closely

Signs, missing powers, the zero of the divisor, and the degree of the quotient.

The big idea

Division is a relationship

For numbers:

107 = 4(26) + 3

For polynomials, the same structure becomes:

P(x) = D(x)Q(x) + R(x)

Here, P(x) is the dividend, D(x) the divisor, Q(x) the quotient, and R(x) the remainder.

Number reminder

The four moves

1Divide

Use the first terms to decide the next quotient term.

2Multiply

Multiply that quotient term by the whole divisor.

3Subtract

Subtract the product carefully, including signs.

4Repeat

Bring down the next part and continue.

Polynomial long division follows exactly the same rhythm.

Bridge

From number division to polynomial division

Now replace the numbers with powers of x.

x3 + 3x2 − 2x + 5 ÷ (x + 1)

The first question is always about the leading terms:

x3 ÷ x = x2

That becomes the first term of the quotient.

Before dividing

Get the polynomial ready

Write the dividend in descending powers.

x4 − 4x2 − 2x + 3

There is no x3 term, so write its coefficient as zero:

x4 + 0x3 − 4x2 − 2x + 3
Do not skip the zero. It keeps every column aligned when you divide or use synthetic division.
Long Division

First worked polynomial division

Divide 3x3 − 5x2 − 7x − 1 by x − 3.

x−3
3x² + 4x + 5
3x³ − 5x² − 7x − 1
3x³ − 9x²
────────
4x² − 7x

4x² − 12x
────────
5x − 1

5x − 15
────────
14

The quotient is 3x² + 4x + 5 and the remainder is 14, matching the supplied lesson example.

Long Division explained

What each step is doing

  1. 3x³ ÷ x = 3x² → first quotient term.
  2. 3x²(x−3)=3x³−9x² → multiply.
  3. Subtract → 4x²−7x.
  4. 4x² ÷ x = 4x → next quotient term.
  5. Repeat until the remainder has smaller degree than the divisor.
Stopping rule: stop when the degree of the remainder is less than the degree of the divisor.
Write the answer properly

Quotient form vs. division statement

For the previous example:

(3x³−5x²−7x−1)/(x−3) = 3x²+4x+5 + 14/(x−3)

The equivalent division statement is:

3x³−5x²−7x−1 = (x−3)(3x²+4x+5)+14

The source lesson explicitly uses both forms and recommends checking by expanding the product.

Check your division

Why verification matters

A correct division can always be reconstructed:

Dividend = Divisor × Quotient + Remainder

For x³+9x²−5x+3 divided by x−2:

x³+9x²−5x+3=(x−2)(x²+11x+17)+37
When you multiply and simplify the right side, you should get the original dividend exactly.

The shortcut

Remainder Theorem

When P(x) is divided by x−b, the remainder is:

P(b)

So if the question asks only for the remainder, you do not need to perform the whole division.

First move: set the divisor equal to zero to find the value of x you need.

This is the exact rule presented in the supplied lesson package.

Worked example

Remainder Theorem example

Find the remainder when P(x)=x⁴−4x²−2x+3 is divided by x+1.

Step 1: x+1=x−(−1), so use P(−1).

P(−1)=1−4+2+3=2

Therefore, the remainder is 2. This is also question 1 in the supplied review.

The non-monic Remainder Theorem case

The supplied teacher lesson also gives the more general case:

Example

Worked non-monic remainder

Find the remainder when P(x)=2x³+x²−3x−6 is divided by 2x−3.

The divisor's zero is 3/2.

P(3/2)=2(3/2)³+(3/2)²−3(3/2)−6
P(3/2)=−3/2

The supplied teacher material verifies this result by long division.

Remainder information

Finding an unknown coefficient

Another source question gives an unknown k and the remainder.

Find k if

P(x)=x⁴+kx³−3x−5

has remainder −10 when divided by x−3.

Use the theorem:

P(3)=−10
The division is not the point here. The point is turning the remainder information into an equation for k.
Worked algebra

Solve for k

3⁴+k(3³)−3(3)−5=−10
81+27k−9−5=−10
27k+67=−10
27k=−77
k=−77/27

This matches the answer in the supplied review solutions.

A shorter layout

Synthetic Division: what changes?

Synthetic division keeps the same core arithmetic as long division, but it works with coefficients rather than the full expressions.

For a divisor of the form x−b, place b to the left and use the coefficient row.

3−5−7−1
Bring down → multiply by 3 → add → repeat.

The supplied lesson gives this process step-by-step.

Worked example

Synthetic division, one move at a time

Divide 3x³−5x²−7x−1 by x−3.

3  |  3    −5    −7    −1
      9    12    15
──────────────
      3     4     5  | 14

The bottom row gives the quotient coefficients 3, 4, 5 and the final number 14 is the remainder.

Read the bottom row

How to rebuild the quotient

The original polynomial has degree 3, so after division by a linear expression, the quotient has degree 2.

3, 4, 5 → 3x²+4x+5

The final number is separate:

R=14

So the division statement is:

3x³−5x²−7x−1=(x−3)(3x²+4x+5)+14
Critical setup

Missing coefficients in synthetic division

For x³−7x−6, there is no x² term.

The coefficient row is:

1, 0, −7, −6

The supplied workbook intentionally includes this kind of problem.

Zero is not optional. It represents the missing power.

Sign control

When the divisor is x + 3

Rewrite:

x+3 = x−(−3)

So synthetic division uses −3 on the left.

Common trap: copy the sign from the divisor without first finding its zero. Do not use +3 here.

The supplied workbook's answer key shows this same sign handling in the examples with x+3 and x+2.

Worked example

Synthetic division with x + 2

Divide x³+2x²−6x+1 by x+2.

Use −2:

−2  |  1    2    −6    1
      −2   0   12
────────────────
      1   0   −6  | 13

Therefore:

x³+2x²−6x+1=(x+2)(x²−6)+13
2x − 3 and similar cases

Non-monic divisors in synthetic division

The supplied lesson explains that a divisor such as 2x+3 can be rewritten first:

2x+3 = 2(x+3/2)

So the synthetic number is the zero of x+3/2, namely −3/2.

After the synthetic work, the quotient must be adjusted for the factor 2. The source explicitly warns that long division may be easier for a divisor ax−b.

Source case

Worked non-monic synthetic example

Divide 6x³+5x²−16x−15 by 2x+3.

2x+3=2(x+3/2)

Use synthetic number −3/2:

−3/2  |  6    5    −16    −15
      −9   6   15
──────────────────
      6   −4   −10  | 0

The synthetic bottom row gives 6x²−4x−10. Divide the quotient by 2:

Q(x)=3x²−2x−5
Remainder = 0, so the divisor is a factor.

Reasoning

A deeper question: why the degree drops

If a degree-5 polynomial is divided by a degree-1 polynomial, the quotient has degree 4.

5 − 1 = 4

This matters when reading a synthetic-division row: if the dividend has degree 4, the first bottom-row value is the coefficient of x³, not x⁴.

Bridge to Factor Theorem

The remainder connects the theorems

For x−b, the Remainder Theorem says the remainder is P(b).

So if

P(b)=0

the remainder is zero, which means x−b divides evenly.

This is the doorway to the Factor Theorem. We are not doing the full factoring lesson here; just connect the ideas.
