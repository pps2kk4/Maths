Degrees vs. Reality

Since childhood, you were told a full circle has 360 degrees.

The Truth

Degrees are made up! The Babylonians based it on the ~360 days in their calendar year. It has no mathematical connection to the actual geometry of a circle.

To do high-level calculus and physics, we need a measurement based on the circle itself.

Enter the Radian

A Radian is a physical measurement.

It is the angle created when you take the radius (r) and wrap it around the curve of the circle.

Circumference = 2πr

Therefore, it takes exactly 2π radians to wrap around a full circle.

The Golden Conversion

If a full circle (360°) is 2π radians, then a half-circle is:

180° = π radians

Degrees to Radians

Multiply by (π / 180°)

Radians to Degrees

Multiply by (180° / π)

Arc Length Formula

Because radians physically wrap the radius around the curve, finding the arc length is simple.

a = r θ
  • a = Arc Length
  • r = Radius
  • θ = Angle

Fatal Exam Trap

The angle θ MUST BE IN RADIANS.

If you plug 60° into this formula instead of π/3, your answer will be completely wrong.

Co-terminal Angles

Angles are co-terminal if they share the exact same terminal arm.

You can find them by adding or subtracting a full circle (2π or 360°).

Original: π / 4

Add one full spin:

π/4 + 2π = π/4 + 8π/4

Co-terminal: 9π / 4

Special Triangles: π / 4

To find exact values without a calculator, we use our Grade 11 triangles, updated for Grade 12.

The 45-45-90 triangle becomes the π/4 - π/4 - π/2 triangle.

  • sin(π/4) = 1 / √2
  • cos(π/4) = 1 / √2
  • tan(π/4) = 1 / 1 = 1

Special Triangles: π / 6 & π / 3

The 30-60-90 triangle becomes the π/6 - π/3 - π/2 triangle.

Tip: The smallest angle (π/6) is always opposite the smallest side (1).

  • sin(π/6) = 1 / 2
  • cos(π/6) = √3 / 2
  • tan(π/3) = √3 / 1 = √3

The Unit Circle & CAST Rule

Any coordinate on the Unit Circle corresponds to (cosθ, sinθ).

Because Cosine is 'x' and Sine is 'y', their signs (+ or -) change depending on the quadrant.

  • Q1: x(+), y(+) → ALL positive
  • Q2: x(-), y(+) → SINE positive
  • Q3: x(-), y(-) → TAN positive (neg/neg)
  • Q4: x(+), y(-) → COS positive

Reciprocal Trig Ratios

Beyond SOH CAH TOA, what if we flip the fractions upside down?

We get the Reciprocal Ratios:

  • Cosecant (csc) = 1 / sin
  • Secant (sec) = 1 / cos
  • Cotangent (cot) = 1 / tan

Exam Tip

Don't mix up the letters! 'S' pairs with 'C'.

Sine ↔ Csc

Cosine ↔ Sec

The Base Waves (Sine & Cosine)

Plotting the y-values (Sine) and x-values (Cosine) from the Unit Circle.

y = sin(x)

y = cos(x)

Both have an amplitude of 1 and a period of 2π.

Graphing Tangent

y = tan(x) behaves very differently.

Because tan(x) = sin(x) / cos(x), whenever cos(x) is 0, we get an asymptote!

  • Vertical Asymptotes at π/2, 3π/2...
  • Period is only π (not 2π)
  • No maximum or minimum amplitude.

Graphing Reciprocals (e.g., Cosecant)

How do we graph y = csc(x)?

Csc is 1/sin(x). The golden rule is simple:

Wherever the original wave equals 0, the reciprocal has a Vertical Asymptote (because 1/0 is undefined).

The reciprocal graphs "bounce" off the peaks and valleys of the original wave.

The Period Formula

Period = 2π / |k|

The Mapping Rule

To graph complex transformations, take the key points of the base wave and apply the mapping rule:

(x, y) → ( x / k + d, ay + c )

Example: y = 3 sin(2(x - π/2)) - 1

→ ( x / 2 + π/2, 3y - 1 )

Modeling the Real World

Why do we study sine and cosine? Because they perfectly model real-world cycles.

If a word problem gives you a Maximum and Minimum value, you can build the equation!

  • Axis (c) = (Max + Min) / 2
  • Amplitude (a) = (Max - Min) / 2
  • k value = 2π / Time for one full cycle
